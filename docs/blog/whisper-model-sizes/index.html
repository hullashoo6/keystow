Whisper Tiny, Base, Small, Large: Which Model Size Do You Need?

November 24, 2026 · KeyStow · All articles

One name, many models

"Whisper" is not one model but a family. OpenAI released the speech-recognition models open-source (MIT license), and they come in sizes: tiny, base, small, medium, large (with later variants like large-v3 and a distilled "turbo"). The size is the number of parameters, and it drives the two things you care about: how accurate the transcription is, and how fast (and on what hardware) it runs.

The trade-off in plain terms

  • Tiny and base run fast, even on an ordinary laptop without a graphics card. Accuracy is good for clear, single-speaker dictation, which is exactly what desk dictation is.
  • Small and medium buy noticeably better accuracy on accents, noise and rare words, at the cost of more memory and slower transcription on modest hardware.
  • Large is the accuracy ceiling, and where cloud services get their best results. On a typical laptop it is heavy; on a desktop with a decent GPU it is practical.

There is no universal best size, only the best size for your machine and your tolerance for corrections.

What this means when choosing a dictation app

Apps make different choices:

  • KeyStow bundles the base model by default (the faster-whisper build) and lets you switch between tiny, base, small, medium, large-v3 and turbo in its settings; sizes beyond the bundled base download once. Base is the deliberate default: quick on ordinary hardware, accurate for hold-to-talk dictation, and small enough to bundle for offline use. $5 once, Windows and Mac, MIT.
  • Superwhisper offers a choice of models across its tiers, including larger ones for paying users (Pro from $8.49 per month).
  • Handy (free, open source) runs local models with options documented in its repository.
  • Wispr Flow transcribes in the cloud (per its privacy page), so its model size is their problem, not yours; the trade is that your audio leaves your machine and you need internet.

A practical rule of thumb

For one person speaking clearly at a desk, base-class models are the sweet spot today. Accuracy depends on your audio, your voice and the model size, and speed depends on your hardware and that same choice. If you dictate specialist vocabulary all day (medicine, law, code names), a larger model earns its keep, either through an app that offers one or by accepting slower transcription. And if a tool will not tell you what it runs or where, that is itself an answer.

Why offline model size matters at all

With cloud dictation you rent whatever model the provider runs, and it can change under you. With local dictation the model is yours: pinned, repeatable, and it works the same offline. KeyStow's approach - your voice processed privately on your own computer, never sent to the cloud, no recordings or transcript history kept - only works because a full speech model fits comfortably on a normal computer. The model sizes above are why that is now practical.
