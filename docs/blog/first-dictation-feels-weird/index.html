Why Your First Dictated Draft Feels Weird (and Why That's Fine)

November 24, 2026 · KeyStow · All articles

The day-one disappointment

Everyone's first dictation session ends the same way: you read the transcript and wince. Half-sentences, "um," a thought that circles twice, a paragraph that goes nowhere. The silent verdict: "I type better than I talk."

You do. Everyone does, at first. You have typed for decades and dictated for an hour. The comparison is rigged.

What is actually happening

  • Typing hides the mess. A typed first draft feels cleaner because it was slow enough to edit mid-sentence. The thinking was always this messy; the keyboard just concealed it.
  • Speaking reveals structure problems early. A paragraph you cannot say smoothly is a paragraph with a logic problem. Dictation surfaces that in the first minute; typing hides it until the third read.
  • The skill arrives fast. Day one is halting, day three is odd but productive, week two is fluent. The learning curve is measured in days, not months.

Working with the weirdness

  • Judge the second draft, not the first. Dictation's deal is: fast rough draft, then a real edit. Grading the raw transcript is grading half the process.
  • Keep the sessions short at first. Ten minutes of dictation, then stop. Stamina and fluency build together.
  • Save your early drafts. Comparing day one to day ten is the motivation that gets you through day four.
