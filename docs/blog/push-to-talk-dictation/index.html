Push-to-Talk vs Always-Listening Dictation: Why Holding a Key Is a Feature

November 24, 2026 · KeyStow · All articles

Two philosophies of dictation

Dictation tools split into two camps. Always-listening tools wait for a wake word or start transcribing the moment you open them, and keep listening until you say stop. Push-to-talk tools do nothing until you hold a key, listen while you hold it, and stop listening the instant you release.

Always-listening sounds more convenient. In practice, for people who write at a computer all day, push-to-talk wins more often than not. Here is why.

Control over what gets heard

With push-to-talk, the microphone is only working while your finger is down. The meeting reminder you mutter, the phone call in the background, the sentence you think better of halfway through: none of it is captured, because you never pressed the key. With always-listening, the tool decides when speech starts and stops, and its guesses become part of your transcript.

Better transcription, oddly enough

Holding the key is also a small mental trigger: you compose the sentence first, then speak it cleanly. That one habit improves both the transcription and the writing. Always-listening encourages thinking out loud, which produces meandering text you then have to untangle.

Privacy by design

Push-to-talk pairs naturally with local processing. When the tool only ever hears deliberate snippets, and those snippets are recognized on your own machine, very little of your life touches the tool at all. KeyStow is built on exactly this combination: hold Right Option (the default, changeable), talk, release, and the text appears in whatever app you are using. Your voice is processed privately on your own computer and never sent to the cloud, dictation works without internet too, and KeyStow does not keep an audio recording or transcript history. It costs $5 once and is MIT open source.

When always-listening does make sense

Fairness demands the cases: if your hands are occupied (cooking, driving, physical therapy), a wake-word tool is the right choice. And some cloud tools use always-on listening to offer conversational, assistant-style features. That is a different product category; it comes with microphones-on-most-of-the-time and, in Wispr Flow's case, a privacy page stating that transcription always happens in the cloud. For sitting-down writing work, the trade is rarely worth it.
