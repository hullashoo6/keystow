How to Choose a Dictation App: A Practical Checklist

November 24, 2026 · KeyStow · All articles

Six questions before you install anything

Dictation apps look identical in screenshots and differ everywhere that matters. Run any candidate through these six questions and the right answer usually falls out.

1. Where does your audio go?

The single most important question. Cloud tools send your voice to their servers; Wispr Flow states it plainly on its privacy page: transcription always happens in the cloud. Local tools recognize speech on your own machine. If you dictate client work, health topics, unreleased projects or just value privacy on principle, local wins. Check the privacy page, not the marketing page.

2. Does it work offline?

A local model means dictation on planes, trains and bad connections, and no dependency on a company's servers staying up. KeyStow, Handy and Superwhisper (local tier) work offline. Windows 11's built-in voice typing does not; Microsoft's support page says it relies on an online speech service.

3. What is the real price model?

  • Free and open source: Handy.
  • One-time: KeyStow at $5 lifetime; Superwhisper offers a $249.99 lifetime plan.
  • Subscription: Wispr Flow Pro at $15 per user per month ($12 annually); Superwhisper Pro from $8.49 per month.

A subscription is not automatically wrong; it buys ongoing development and, often, cloud features. But over three years $12 a month is $432. Know which product you are buying.

4. Does it type where you actually write?

Your writing happens in email, docs, chat, your CMS, your IDE. A system-wide tool that types into any focused app (KeyStow, Handy, Superwhisper, Wispr Flow all work this way) beats a per-app plugin. Check your platform too: Windows and Apple Silicon Mac are covered by KeyStow; Handy adds Linux; Wispr Flow adds iPhone and Android.

5. How does it listen?

Hold-to-talk (press and hold a key while you speak) gives you exact control over what is heard and tends to produce cleaner takes. Always-listening and wake-word tools are more convenient when your hands are busy, but the tool decides when to listen. KeyStow uses hold-to-talk with a changeable key.

6. Can you verify its claims?

Open-source tools let you check what they actually do. KeyStow and Handy are MIT-licensed; their repositories are public. For closed tools you are trusting the privacy page. Trust, but prefer verify.

A worked example

Run KeyStow through the checklist: local Whisper models (1, 2), $5 once (3), any app on Windows and Apple Silicon Mac (4), hold-to-talk with a changeable key (5), MIT open source (6). Your voice is processed privately on your own computer and never sent to the cloud, and no audio recording or transcript history is kept. That is what "passes the checklist" looks like; use the same six questions on whatever else you are considering.
