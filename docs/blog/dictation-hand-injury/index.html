Writing With a Hand Injury: Dictation When You Can't Type

November 24, 2026 · KeyStow · All articles

Two hands are a luxury you notice when one is gone

Sprained wrist, broken finger, arm in a sling: suddenly every email is a chore and every document a project. Work does not pause for a splint, and pecking one-handed at a full inbox is miserable.

Dictation is the practical bridge. It is not a medical treatment and it will not heal anything; it simply moves the typing out of the way while your hand does whatever healing hands do.

Setting up a one-handed workflow

  • Email and messages first. These are the daily volume. Dictate replies; keep them a touch shorter than usual and nobody will notice anything but your responsiveness.
  • Documents second. Drafts by voice, then ask a colleague or a free afternoon for the formatting pass.
  • Push-to-talk beats always-on here. When your movement is limited, fumbling to stop an always-listening tool is its own annoyance. A hold-to-talk key listens exactly while you hold it and not a word more.
  • Be honest about limits. Heavy spreadsheet or design work will still be slow. Dictation covers the writing; it does not make you ambidextrous.

A note on going back

Most people return to the keyboard when the cast comes off, and that is fine. Many keep dictation for first drafts afterward, having discovered under duress that speaking a draft is faster than typing one. Either outcome is a win.

How KeyStow fits

KeyStow is an offline voice dictation app for Windows and Apple Silicon Mac. Hold Right Ctrl on Windows (Right Option on a Mac, and changeable to a key your working hand can reach), speak, release, and the text is typed where your cursor is. Your voice is processed privately on your own computer and never sent to the cloud, and dictation works without internet too. KeyStow does not keep an audio recording or transcript history.
