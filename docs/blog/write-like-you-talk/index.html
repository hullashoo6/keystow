Write Like You Talk: How Dictation Changes Your Voice (and When to Stop It)

November 24, 2026 · KeyStow · All articles

The best writing advice, mechanized

"Write like you talk" is the oldest style advice there is, and dictation makes it literal. When you speak a draft, you cannot hide behind typed tics: no passive-voice thickets, no filler phrases you would never say aloud, no sentences too long to say in one breath, because you literally need the breath. Spoken drafts come out clearer, warmer and more direct by default.

That is usually a feature. Not always.

When the spoken voice wins

  • Emails, blog posts, newsletters, marketing: anywhere a human is reading and you want them to feel talked with, not written at.
  • Explaining anything complicated: if you can say it to a colleague, you can dictate it, and the explanation will be better structured than your typed attempt.
  • First drafts of everything: momentum matters more than polish at the start.

When to override it

  • Legal, academic and technical writing: some genres require precision constructions that speech does not naturally produce. Dictate the argument, then formalize in the edit.
  • Spoken tics: everyone has verbal habits ("so basically", trailing qualifiers) that read fine aloud and badly on the page. They are easy to cut once you know yours.
  • Rhythm: speech varies sentence length naturally, but a fully dictated page can drift into uniform chattiness. Read the edit aloud again; your ear is the editor.

A workflow that controls it

Dictate the draft. Edit once for transcription artifacts. Then do one read specifically for register: is this sentence something I would say to this reader, in this genre? That single question catches both directions: passages too stiff (re-speak them) and passages too loose (tighten them).
