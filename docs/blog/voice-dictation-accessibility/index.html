Voice Dictation for Accessibility: Writing With Far Fewer Keystrokes

November 24, 2026 · KeyStow · All articles

When every keystroke costs something

For most people dictation is a speed upgrade. For others it is a work-around for a real limitation: repetitive strain injury, carpal tunnel syndrome, arthritis, an injury, or a motor impairment can make sustained typing painful or impossible. Dictation does not remove the keyboard from your life, but it can remove most of the keystrokes from your writing. Speech recognition has served this role for decades; tools like Dragon built their reputations as assistive technology long before dictation was a productivity trend.

What dictation replaces, and what it does not

Being precise matters here. A hold-to-talk dictation tool replaces the long stretches of prose typing: sentences, paragraphs, emails, documents. It does not replace everything. You still hold a shortcut key while you speak (KeyStow's default is Right Ctrl on Windows, Right Option on a Mac, and you can change it to whatever is most comfortable). You still click into the app you are using. And the editing pass - fixing a name, moving a sentence - is usually still keyboard and mouse work.

So the honest claim is "far fewer keystrokes", not "hands-free". If you need fully hands-free computer use, look at your operating system's voice control alongside dictation: Windows 11's Voice Access can drive the interface itself (clicking, scrolling, opening apps) and is free and built in. Apple has a similar Voice Control feature on Mac. Pairing OS voice control for navigation with a dictation tool for prose gets closest to a hands-free setup.

What to look for

  • A changeable shortcut key. Comfort is personal. Pick the key that is kindest to your hands; KeyStow lets you change it.
  • Works in any app. Pain does not care whether you are in email, a document or chat. A system-wide tool that types into any focused app avoids juggling per-app features.
  • Offline processing. If dictation is how you write everything, everything you write passes through it. KeyStow's Whisper models run locally: your voice is processed privately on your own computer and never sent to the cloud, dictation works without internet too, and no audio recording or transcript history is kept.
  • A fair price. Accessibility tools have a history of enterprise pricing (Dragon Professional v16 is $699). KeyStow is $5 once, lifetime. Handy is free and open source.

The realistic options

  • KeyStow ($5 once, lifetime): offline, hold-to-talk with a changeable key, types into any app, Windows and Mac, MIT open source.
  • Handy (free, MIT): offline push-to-talk for Windows, macOS and Linux.
  • Windows Voice Access and Apple Voice Control: free, built-in hands-free control of the computer itself; complement dictation rather than replace it.
  • Windows voice typing (Win+H) and Apple Dictation: free and built in; Microsoft's page notes Win+H uses an online service, and Apple's notes on-device processing depends on the Mac and settings.
  • Superwhisper (free local tier, Pro from $8.49 per month) and Wispr Flow (free tier, Pro $15 per user per month, cloud-based): paid alternatives.

Setting up a lower-strain workflow

1. Install your dictation tool and spend ten minutes choosing the hold key. Test several before settling.

2. Dictate in short, relaxed takes of a sentence or two. There is no prize for long holds.

3. Learn the few keyboard shortcuts you use most in your main apps, or use OS voice control for the clicking and scrolling between dictation takes.

4. Edit in whatever way is comfortable that day.

One important note

Software can reduce how much you type. It cannot tell you why typing hurts or how to treat it. If typing is painful, a medical professional's assessment is worth more than any tool on this list.
