Voice Dictation for Journalists: Drafting Stories Faster After the Interview

November 24, 2026 · KeyStow · All articles

The writing after the interview is the bottleneck

Recording and transcribing interviews is a solved, separate problem (this article is not about that; always follow consent and recording laws). The slow part most reporters actually complain about comes after: turning a head full of quotes and context into a coherent story on deadline. That is where dictation helps: you already know what the story is. Saying it is faster than typing it.

A deadline workflow that holds up

1. Dump the skeleton. Right after the interview, dictate the story's shape from memory: the lede you heard in your head on the way back, the three quotes you know matter, the nut graf. Five minutes of talking beats thirty minutes of staring.

2. Verify against your notes and recording. Quotes must be exact. Dictation drafts your narration; your notes supply the quotes, word for word.

3. Dictate the body in sections. Talk through one section at a time as if briefing a colleague.

4. Edit by keyboard. Structure, tightening, and quote placement are keyboard work. Dictation gets the clay on the table; editing sculpts it.

Why offline matters to journalists specifically

Reporters handle embargoed information, sensitive source material and unpublished stories. A dictation tool that streams your voice to the cloud is a third party hearing your story before publication. Habits that make dictation stick on deadline

  • Dictate the lede three times. Out loud, in a row, picking the best. Faster than rewriting one lede three times.
  • Name your punctuation while drafting if it helps, or clean it in the edit; pick one convention and stay with it.
  • Keep names and spellings for the edit pass. Unusual names may need keyboard correction; do not let that interrupt your flow mid-draft.
