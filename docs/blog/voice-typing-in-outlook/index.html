Voice Typing in Outlook: Built-in Dictate vs an Offline Option

November 24, 2026 · KeyStow · All articles

Two ways to speak your emails

Microsoft 365's Outlook includes a Dictate button that transcribes your speech into the message body. It is convenient and built in. The trade-off is architectural: Microsoft's own documentation describes its dictation features as cloud-based services, so your audio leaves your PC and an internet connection is required.

The alternative is an offline dictation app that types into any focused field, including Outlook's compose window, with the speech model running on your own computer.

How they compare for email

  • Connection. Built-in Dictate needs internet. Offline dictation keeps working on planes, trains and dead zones, and your drafts are ready when the connection returns.
  • Privacy. Built-in Dictate sends audio to Microsoft's cloud. KeyStow processes your voice on your own computer and never sends it to the cloud, and keeps no audio recording or transcript history.
  • Languages and features. Microsoft's service supports many languages and voice commands. KeyStow transcribes English and focuses on one thing: accurate text at the cursor.
  • Cost. Dictate comes with a Microsoft 365 subscription. KeyStow costs $5 once, with no subscription and no account.

Which fits your inbox

If you already pay for Microsoft 365, work online, and dictate short routine replies, the built-in button is right there. If your email contains client-confidential material, if you travel, or if you simply prefer your voice to stay on your PC, offline dictation is the better fit.

How KeyStow fits

KeyStow is an offline voice dictation app for Windows and Mac. Hold Right Ctrl on Windows (Right Option on a Mac), speak, release, and the text is pasted into the message you are writing. The speech model ships inside the installer, so there is no model download on first use. The source code is open under the MIT license.

It costs $5 once, with no subscription and no account.
