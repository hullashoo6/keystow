How to Dictate Emails by Voice on Windows

November 24, 2026 · KeyStow · All articles

The inbox is a typing tax

Most emails are short, but there are dozens of them. Replies, follow-ups, quick updates: each one is a minute or two of typing, and together they eat the morning. Dictation cuts that down because replies are exactly the kind of writing we do naturally out loud. You already know what you would say if the person called; dictation just writes it down.

How it works with KeyStow

KeyStow is a small Windows app (also on Mac) that types into whatever program is focused. The flow for email:

1. Open your mail app: Outlook, Gmail in the browser, Thunderbird, anything with a text field.

2. Click into the reply box as usual.

3. Hold your shortcut (Right Ctrl on Windows, Right Option on a Mac by default; you can change it), speak your reply, release. The transcribed text appears at the cursor.

4. Glance over it, fix a name if needed, send.

Recognition runs locally with Whisper speech models: your voice is processed privately on your own computer and never sent to the cloud, and dictation works without internet too. That matters doubly for email, which is where contracts, HR topics and client details live. KeyStow does not keep an audio recording or transcript history. It costs $5 once, is MIT open source, and starts at sign-in so it is always there.

Tips for good dictated email

  • Speak in complete sentences, at conversation speed. Modern models handle natural speech well; spelling out punctuation is unnecessary because the model adds it from your phrasing, and you can adjust in the edit.
  • Keep it to one thought per hold. A sentence or three per press works better than a five-minute monologue.
  • Do names and numbers on the keyboard. Unusual names, addresses and figures are faster to type than to correct.
  • Use it for the first 80%. Dictate the reply, then tidy the greeting and sign-off by hand.

The built-in option, and its limits

Windows 11 has voice typing built in: press Win+H in any text field. It costs nothing and is fine for a quick test. Microsoft's support page notes it relies on an online speech service, so it needs internet and sends audio to Microsoft's cloud. For occasional short emails on a good connection it works; for a full inbox or anything sensitive, a local tool is the better fit.

Other tools

  • Handy (free, MIT): offline push-to-talk for Windows, macOS and Linux. Genuinely free.
  • Wispr Flow (free tier, Pro $15 per user per month): cloud-based with automatic cleanup and formatting; its privacy page says transcription always happens in the cloud.
  • Superwhisper (free local tier, Pro from $8.49 per month): local processing with more modes.
