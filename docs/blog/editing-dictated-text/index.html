How to Edit a Voice-Drafted Document (Without Losing the Speed You Gained)

November 24, 2026 · KeyStow · All articles

Dictation gets you a draft, not a document

The deal with voice dictation is simple: you trade typing speed for an editing pass. Speak at 130-plus words per minute, get a rough draft, then spend focused time fixing it. Writers who quit dictation usually skip the system for the second half and decide the draft is "too messy." The mess is predictable, which means you can edit for it on purpose.

The three-pass edit

Pass 1: Structure (read, don't fix). Read the whole draft once. Move paragraphs, cut tangents, note missing pieces. Dictated drafts ramble in a way typed drafts don't: you said things because they occurred to you, in that order. Reordering is normal and fast.

Pass 2: Transcription artifacts. This is the pass typing never needs. Hunt for:

  • Homophones and near-misses: their/there, your/you're, and whatever soundalikes live in your vocabulary.
  • Punctuation gaps: even if you speak punctuation, commas go missing. Read for rhythm.
  • Names and jargon: product names, people, technical terms. Speech models guess; you know the truth.
  • "Um" echoes: speech models filter most fillers, but spoken habits ("so basically") sneak in as wordy openers. Cut them.

Pass 3: The normal edit. Tighten sentences, check flow, add the example you remembered mid-shower. This is the editing you already know.

Keep the speed gain

Two rules protect the time you saved:

1. Do not edit while dictating. Stopping mid-sentence to fix a word kills the flow that made dictation fast. Mark it, keep talking, fix in pass 2.

2. Timebox the cleanup. If the editing pass takes longer than typing would have, your drafts are too loose. Tighten your speaking, not your editing.
